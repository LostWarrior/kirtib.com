<p>We have all lived by adage: “Pick two: Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance.” or as it is widely known CAP theorem. However, I find that this framing is misleading. In a real distributed system you do not get to <code>choose</code> partition tolerance. Networks can fail whether you like it or not, it is just a fact of life that you must handle.<br><br>A better framing is: <span data-color="#134f5c" style="color: rgb(19, 79, 92);">When a partition happens, you must choose: consistency or availability.  When there is no partition, choose consistency or latency.</span></p><p>Now, let’s talk about both scenarios:</p><ol><li><p><strong>Spock Mode</strong>: During partition, you chose consistency (CP)</p></li></ol><p>If you can not confirm answer with others, you refuse to answer instead of giving wrong or stale responses. <br><br><em>Price Paid</em>: During a failure, part of your system goes dark<br><br><em>Examples</em>:<br>a) Banking: anything to do with bank account, payments, transfers etc. You don’t want two partitions deciding same $100 can be spent.<br>b) Uniqueness constraint: If uniqueness matters, you may need consensus before accepting a write. e.g, usernames, ids etc.<br>c)  Anything with limited numbers (concert tickets, hotel rooms), state machines, permissions etc.</p><ol start="2"><li><p><strong>Captain Kirk Mode</strong>: During partition, you chose availability (AP)</p></li></ol><p>You answer even if your might have old/incomplete information . This is preferable when downtime is worse than being correct.<br><br><em>Price Paid</em>: You must handle conflicts.</p><p><em>Examples</em>: </p><p>a) Social Media: Something showing up a couple of seconds late is better than a blank feed.</p><p>b) Shopping Carts:  Amazon famously adopted this in its original Dynamo architecture for shopping cart, favouring availability and partition tolerance over immediate consistency. Amazon won't block you from adding an item just because a database in another part of the country can't confirm it yet.<br></p><h5><strong>Latency or Consistency</strong></h5><p>Okay, now that we have partition mode covered. What happens when there are smooth waters. This brings us back to our original framing and second part/extension of CAP theorem, also known as PACELC. In normal times, you have two choices choose <em>latency</em> or <em>consistency</em>. </p><p><em>Let’s take an example: </em><br><br>Imagine a write replicated across: London —&gt; Seattle —&gt; Singapore. Should London node wait for all replicas to confirm write or acknowledge immediately and replicate async? This decision is taken based on the data, operation and multiple other factors.<br><br>1) Your system says give me the fastest reasonable answer. I don’t need every replica to confirm it first. For example, news feed or social media feed. If a post from 200 ms ago is missing, that’s fine. It is generally alright for these systems to be built keeping latency in mind. </p><p>2)  Verify state is correct even if it takes a bit longer. For example, your bank balance, access tokens (imagine revoking someone’s access and they are able to get in because we did not check), usernames, last concert tickets etc.<br><br>This is all fine in theory but, in real world you will have much better results from saying, check at least three replicas for a payment and one replica is good enough for profile picture, in the same database.<br><br>So, essentially CAP tells you what you need to sacrifice when things break. PACELC (quite a mouthful) tells you that tradeoffs exists even when things are working fine.<br><br>Before designing such a system, some good questions to ask are:<br><br>1. During partition which operations must remain available?<br>2. Which data must never have conflicting versions?<br>3. What is acceptable latency before it impacts user experience?<br>4. What should application do when replicas disagree?<br>5. Does an operation require coordination across regions?<br>6. Are consistency requirements the same for reads and writes?<br><br>A good rule of thumb is pay for <em>consistency</em> when stale data triggers something irreversible. Choose <em>latency</em> everywhere else. </p><p>In next post we will talk about consistency patterns.</p>
